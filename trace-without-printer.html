How to trace a picture onto paper without a printer

Five methods, what each costs, and which to use.

You have a picture on your phone and a blank sheet of paper. You do not have a printer, tracing paper, or a light box. Here is how people actually solve this, from the cheapest to the most reliable.

1. The window

The oldest trick there is. Tape your reference to a window in daylight, tape the blank paper over it, and the light coming through makes the lines visible. Trace them.

Good: free, works immediately.
Bad: you need a printed reference, so this one does not help if your picture is only on a phone. Your arms ache after ten minutes, and it only works in daylight.

2. The phone-under-glass method

Put your phone on a table, screen up, brightness at maximum, and lay the paper over it. On thin paper the image shows through well enough to trace.

Good: free, no printing.
Bad: your phone is about 15 cm wide, so you are limited to small drawings. The paper must be thin. Your hand presses buttons on the screen. Many people try this once and give up.

3. The grid method

Draw a grid of squares over your reference, draw the same grid lightly on your paper, and copy the contents of each square one at a time. This is not tracing at all — it is a transfer technique, and it is centuries old.

Good: free, works at any size, and it genuinely teaches you to see shapes rather than objects.
Bad: slow. Setting up the grid takes longer than most beginners expect, and the pencil grid lines need erasing afterwards.

4. Carbon or graphite transfer paper

Buy a sheet of graphite transfer paper, put it between your printed reference and your page, and draw over the lines. The pressure transfers graphite onto the sheet below.

Good: accurate, works on thick paper and canvas.
Bad: you have to buy it, and you still need a printed copy of the image — which brings the printer back.

5. Your phone's camera, held above the paper

Instead of putting the picture under the paper, put the phone above it. The camera looks down at the page, the screen shows your paper with the picture laid over it, and you draw on the real paper while watching the screen.

This is what augmented-reality tracing apps do. On phones that support ARCore the picture is pinned to the paper itself, so you can move the phone, put it down, and come back later with the image still in exactly the same place at the same size.

Good: no printing, no tracing paper, no size limit, and it works with any photo already on your phone.
Bad: you need something to hold the phone steady — a stand, a stack of books, or a desk lamp clamp. Drawing while looking at a screen takes about ten minutes to get used to.

Which to choose

For a one-off small drawing on thin paper, the phone-under-glass trick is fine. For anything larger, repeated, or from a photo on your phone, the camera method is the only one that does not involve a printer at some stage.

A note on paper

Whatever method you use, smooth paper traces better than textured paper. Cartridge paper around 120 gsm is a good middle ground: thick enough not to buckle, smooth enough that the pencil does not catch. Very cheap copier paper works for practice and tears easily under an eraser.
