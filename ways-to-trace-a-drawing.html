5 ways to trace a drawing, compared

Cost, accuracy, and what each method is actually good for.

Every method of getting an image onto paper trades cost against convenience. Here they are side by side, then the detail.

MethodCostSize limitBest for
WindowFreeAny A one-off when you already have a printout and it is daytime
Light box£20–60Box size Repeat work, comics, inking over pencils
GridFreeAny Learning to see shapes, scaling a small image up large
Transfer paper£5–15Any Thick paper, canvas, wood — anything light cannot pass through
Phone cameraFreeAny Photos already on your phone, no printer available

The window

Tape the reference to a sunlit window, tape paper over it, trace. Costs nothing and needs no equipment. You need a printed reference and daylight, and your arms will ache within ten minutes. Fine once, miserable repeatedly.

The light box

A flat panel that lights up from underneath. This is what professional comic artists use for inking, and the cheap LED ones are genuinely good now.

It is the most comfortable method by a distance — flat on a desk, consistent light, no shaking. Its limits are that you still need a printed reference, it only works on reasonably thin paper, and you cannot trace anything bigger than the panel.

The grid

Divide the reference into squares, draw matching squares on your paper, copy square by square. Strictly this is copying rather than tracing, and that is the point: it is the only method on this list that actively teaches you to see.

It also scales. A postcard-sized reference can become a poster, which no light box can do. The cost is time — expect to spend a third of the session just setting up.

Transfer paper

A sheet coated in graphite. Place it face-down between the reference and your surface, draw over the lines, and the pressure deposits graphite below.

This is the method to use when light cannot pass through your surface: canvas, board, thick watercolour paper, wood. It is accurate and repeatable. You need a printed reference, and the transferred line is soft and needs going over properly afterwards.

The phone camera

Rather than putting light behind the paper, you put a camera above it. The phone looks down at your page, its screen shows the page with the image overlaid, and you draw on the real paper while watching the screen.

The weakness of simple versions is that the picture is stuck to the screen, so it slides out of alignment every time your hand nudges the phone. Augmented-reality versions fix this by anchoring the image to the paper itself, so the picture stays put when the phone moves.

It needs no printer, no tracing paper and no daylight, and it works with any photo already on your phone. You do need something to hold the phone steady, and the first ten minutes of drawing while looking at a screen feel strange.

If you can only pick one

Already have a printer and draw a lot? Buy a light box. No printer, pictures on your phone? Use the camera method. Want to actually get better rather than just produce a drawing? Use the grid.
