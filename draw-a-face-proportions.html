How to draw a face using proportions

Where the features actually go, and why your drawings look off.

When a drawn face looks wrong but you cannot say why, it is nearly always proportion rather than skill. The features are drawn well and placed badly. Here is where they actually go.

The basic face grid

Start with an oval, slightly narrower at the chin than at the top. Then:

  • The eyes sit halfway down. Not two thirds, not a third — halfway. This surprises almost everyone, because we think of the face as the part below the hairline and forget how much skull sits above the eyes.
  • The bottom of the nose is halfway between the eyes and the chin.
  • The mouth is roughly a third of the way from the nose to the chin — closer to the nose than to the chin.
  • The face is five eyes wide, with one eye-width of gap between the two eyes.
  • The ears run from the eye line to the bottom of the nose.

Those five rules fix the majority of beginner faces on their own. Draw the lines lightly before you draw a single feature.

The eight-heads rule for the body

For a full figure, the head is the measuring unit. A standard adult is about seven and a half heads tall; heroic and fashion illustration stretches it to eight, which is where the name comes from.

  • Head: 1
  • Chin to nipple line: 1
  • To the navel: 1
  • To the hip: 1
  • To mid-thigh: 1
  • To the knee: 1
  • To mid-calf: 1
  • To the floor: 1

Anime proportions vary deliberately: chibi characters are two to four heads tall, which is why they read as cute. Children are genuinely around five heads — not small adults, but differently proportioned ones, with larger heads relative to the body.

The fastest fix

If your figures look stumpy, your legs are too short. The halfway point of a standing adult body is the hip, not the waist. Measure it on yourself.

Three-quarter views

The moment the head turns, two things change and most people only remember one:

  1. The far eye becomes narrower and sits closer to the edge of the face.
  2. The centre line of the face — down the nose, through the middle of the mouth — curves. It is not straight any more, because it is wrapping around a sphere.

Drawing that centre line as a curve before placing any feature is the single most useful habit for three-quarter heads.

How to practise it

Draw ten blank ovals on a sheet. Put the grid lines on all ten before you draw any features. Then fill them in. Doing the measurement ten times in a row is what makes it automatic — doing it once in the middle of a drawing you care about does not.

A proportion guide you can trace over is useful here for a different reason than it first appears: not to produce a finished drawing, but to put the correct spacing into your hand so that you start placing features there without measuring.
